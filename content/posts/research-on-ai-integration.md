Decisions about classroom technology should be based on evidence, not excitement. This article collects the research our team found most useful while designing the program, along with what we think it means for schools.

## Forty years of tutoring research

The story starts with Benjamin Bloom's 1984 paper on the "2 sigma problem," which reported that students who were tutored one-on-one did dramatically better than students in a regular class. Schools could never afford a tutor for every student, so researchers began building computer tutors to fill the gap.

In 2011, Kurt VanLehn reviewed decades of studies comparing human tutors with computer tutoring systems. He found that human tutoring was less powerful than Bloom's first estimate, with an effect size of about 0.79 compared with no tutoring. Computer tutors that gave feedback on each step of a problem were almost as good, at about 0.76. The lesson for schools is that the design matters more than the technology. A tutor that checks every step beats one that only grades the final answer.

## Student use is rising fast

Students are not waiting for permission. Pew Research Center surveys found that the share of US teens using ChatGPT for schoolwork went from 13 percent in 2023 to 26 percent in 2024. Teens were much more comfortable using it to research a new topic than to write an essay. In other words, many students already sense the difference between AI that helps them learn and AI that does the work for them. Schools can build on that instinct instead of fighting it.

## Teachers are finding real time savings

A 2025 Gallup and Walton Family Foundation survey of more than 2,000 public school teachers found that about 60 percent used AI tools for work during the school year. Weekly users said they saved close to six hours a week. For a profession where burnout is a constant concern, that is a meaningful number.

## What this means for integration

Putting this research together, three principles stand out. First, AI tutoring should work step by step and ask for reasoning, not just check answers. Second, schools should give students a safe, school-approved tool, because students will use AI either way. Third, the biggest benefit for teachers comes from automating the routine work, like grouping students and writing reports, so they can spend more time teaching.

Those three principles are the foundation of AI Innovation Lab K-12. For more recent findings, see [AI in K-12: The Numbers](/research/ai-k12-numbers/).
