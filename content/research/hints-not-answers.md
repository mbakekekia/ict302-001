It seems obvious that a student with an AI helper would learn more. A careful study from 2025 shows that it depends entirely on what kind of help the AI gives.

## The research

Bastani and colleagues (2025) ran a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey. Students practiced math in one of three ways: with a standard ChatGPT-style chatbot, with an AI tutor designed with teacher input to give hints instead of answers, or with only their textbook and notes. Afterward, every student took an exam without any AI.

During practice, both AI groups did much better than the textbook group. Students with the standard chatbot solved 48% more practice problems correctly, and students with the hint-giving tutor solved 127% more. But on the exam, the picture flipped. Students who had practiced with the standard chatbot scored 17% lower than students who never used AI. Students who had used the hint-giving tutor scored about the same as the no-AI group, so the harm disappeared. The researchers also found that students who used AI tended to overestimate how much they had learned.

The lesson is that AI which hands students the answer can act as a crutch. It makes practice feel easier while learning quietly drops.

Source: [Bastani, H., et al. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 122*, e2422633122](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html). See also the [Wharton summary of the study](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/).

## What it means for AI Innovation Lab K-12

This study is the main reason the platform's AI is built to guide, not answer. In [AI Learning Games](/how-it-works/learning-games/), hints come in three levels, and none of them give away the final answer. Teachers can [review and block any hint](/educators/teacher-controls/) that gives away too much. Students also have to explain their reasoning in their own words before a skill counts as mastered, which helps fix the overconfidence the researchers found: a student who cannot explain an answer finds out right away that they have not learned it yet.
