Schools are right to be careful about new technology. The research on AI in education is still growing, but some findings are already strong enough to plan around. This page summarizes the evidence that shaped how the program was designed.

## One-on-one help works, and computers can provide some of it

In 1984, the education researcher Benjamin Bloom reported that students tutored one-on-one did far better than students taught in a regular classroom. He called this the "2 sigma problem": tutoring clearly works, but no school can afford a human tutor for every student. Later research found the real gap was smaller than Bloom's original estimate, but still large.

A widely cited 2011 review by Kurt VanLehn compared human tutors with computer tutoring systems. Human tutoring raised learning by an effect size of about 0.79 compared with no tutoring. Intelligent tutoring systems that give feedback on each step of a problem came in at about 0.76, nearly as effective. The key was the step-by-step feedback. Systems that only checked the final answer did much worse. That is why the program's tutor works on each step and asks students to explain their reasoning.

## Students are already using AI

According to the Pew Research Center, 26 percent of US teens said they had used ChatGPT for schoolwork in 2024, double the 13 percent from 2023. Most teens thought it was fine to use it for researching a new topic, but far fewer thought it was acceptable for writing essays. Students are going to use AI either way. A tool built for school, with guardrails and teacher oversight, is a better option than leaving them to figure it out alone.

## Teachers get time back

A 2025 survey by Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation of more than 2,000 US public school teachers found that about 60 percent had used AI tools for their work during the school year. Teachers who used AI every week reported saving close to six hours a week, mostly on preparing lessons, making materials, and handling paperwork. The program aims at the same kind of savings by grouping students and writing progress reports automatically.

## The limits matter too

AI tools can make mistakes, reflect bias in their training data, and make it easier to skip the hard part of learning. The program is built around those risks. The tutor is limited to the lesson, teachers can override anything, and students have to show their thinking. Read more in [Combating Academic Dishonesty](/posts/combating-academic-dishonesty/).
